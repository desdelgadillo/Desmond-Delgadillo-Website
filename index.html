About

My work starts with the learner: I teach blind and low-vision adults to use assistive technology with confidence, building real skills for the tasks they need to do every day.

Most of my work is training — one-to-one and with teams — and building coursework that blind students can actually use. As a web accessibility freelancer, I also conduct WCAG and Section 508 compliance audits for corporate and non-profit clients, testing with the screen readers I use daily and writing remediation guidance developers can act on.

My background in communications, my graduate study in assistive technology, and my own experience as a screen-reader user shape how I teach: training starts from what the learner needs to do — not from a feature tour — and moves at the learner's pace, explained in plain language.

How training works

Training is built around your life and your work — not a generic syllabus. Here's what to expect, whether you're learning on your own or arranging training for your team.

  1. 1

    We start with a conversation

    We talk about what you want to be able to do: the everyday tasks that matter to you — email, documents, spreadsheets, your phone, the apps your job uses — where you're getting stuck, and what you're already using, whether that's JAWS, NVDA, VoiceOver, or TalkBack.

  2. 2

    A plan built around your real tasks

    I put together a training plan based on those goals, starting from your current comfort level. We prioritize the tasks you'll use first, so every session connects to something real in your day.

  3. 3

    Hands-on sessions, at your pace

    We work through your tasks together, step by step, with time to practice and ask questions. You build the keystrokes and habits yourself — I guide, you do — in English or Spanish.

  4. 4

    Follow-through as you build independence

    Between and after sessions, you keep practicing with clear next steps. We check in on what's clicking, revisit anything that isn't, and add new skills as your confidence grows.

Call Recording and Siri Make Capturing Information So Much Easier in iOS 27

By Desmond Delgadillo

Something pretty cool happened tonight. I needed to reschedule a medical appointment. Don't worry; that wasn't the cool part.

I called the medical office and learned that the department I needed was closed for the evening. The representative who was helping me offered to give me the direct line so I could call back tomorrow. This sounded ideal because I had to wade through no less than seven menus before I got connected to this rep. It did not sound ideal because I had nothing to write with.

It was at this point, dear reader, that my inner-nerd activated.

"Would you mind if I record the call so I can capture the number?" I asked, ready for the sting of rejection or even to be hung up on. It's happened.

Instead, I got a hesitant yes. "I'll take it!" I thought.

I turned on the built-in call recording in the iOS Phone app and had him repeat the number and the hours. When the call ended, I didn't open Contacts. I didn't hunt for an "add number" button or try to type digits I half-heard into the right fields. I asked Siri to look at my most recent call transcript and save the new number as a contact.

And do you know what happened?

It did it!

No "I'm sorry, I didn't get that."

No half-baked attempts.

One minute I didn't have my doctor's phone number, and the next minute, I did, and it happened with a microscopic amount of effort, the kind of effort that deserves to be studied in a lab.

It's my job to take apart cool technology and find ways it can help people. So you can trust me when I tell you that this is a feature worth teaching your grandparents at Thanksgiving.

To unpack this as an assistive technology feature, let's think about a blind user's typical version of my original task. Someone reads you a phone number once, at their pace. You either memorize it, fumble for a note-taking app mid-call and miss the first four numbers, or ask them to repeat it, ideally as slowly as possible because despite owning a smartphone since junior high you have never been able to type any faster. Suddenly you are Jason Statham and the fate of your mission is depending on you and your ability to do data entry. Instead, I was able to put two parts of the Apple ecosystem together and get the information I needed it where I needed it.

That is what good accessibility actually looks like. Not a special "Accessibility mode." Not a separate, lesser app. Just a strategic chain of mainstream features that make life easier.

Of course, this only works because the pieces live in one ecosystem; hand any link in that chain to a third-party app and the magic usually dies.

But tonight, the chain held.

The best assistive technology is the kind you stop noticing. Tonight, I noticed.

How to try this yourself

You need an iPhone running a current version of iOS with Apple Intelligence available, since the transcript step runs on that. Everything below uses built-in apps — no downloads.

  1. Make your call as normal from the Phone app. You can start recording before the key moment or partway through, when you realize the information matters.
  2. Turn on call recording. While the call is in progress, tap the call recording button on the call screen (with VoiceOver, it is announced as the call recording button in the in-call controls). Everyone on the call hears an announcement that the call is being recorded — that is built in, and it is not a substitute for asking permission yourself. Ask, the way I did, before you rely on it.
  3. Ask for the information to be repeated slowly and clearly. Names spelled out and numbers read in groups make a dramatically better transcript than the same information spoken once at speed.
  4. End the call and give it a moment. The recording is saved and transcribed automatically; the transcript appears with the call in your Phone app's Recents, and the audio is kept in the Notes app.
  5. Check the transcript before you trust it. Open the call in Recents and read through the text with VoiceOver. Pay special attention to the digits of the phone number — one wrong number makes the contact useless. Fix anything that looks wrong by editing the contact after step 6.
  6. Ask Siri to make the contact. Use plain language, for example: "Siri, look at my most recent call transcript and add [name] as a contact." Siri pulls the name and number from the transcript and creates the contact.
  7. Verify the contact. Ask Siri to read the contact back to you, or open it in Contacts and review each field — name, number, and any hours or notes you asked to include.

Two practical notes. Call recording availability and consent laws vary by place; when in doubt, ask the other person, exactly as you would want them to ask you. And if Siri cannot find the transcript, check that the recording actually finished transcribing — on a long call it can take a minute or two after you hang up.
