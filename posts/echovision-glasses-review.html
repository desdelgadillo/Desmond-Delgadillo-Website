My Time with the Agiga EchoVision Glasses: Brilliant Ideas, Buried Under Buttons

By Desmond Delgadillo

I recently had the chance to test drive the EchoVision smart glasses from Agiga, and I wanted to share my takeaways with you in case you're considering dropping upwards of $600 just to try these guys.

Agiga have a tremendous vision of what their EchoVision glasses can be for the blind and visually impaired community: Take the utility of smart glasses like Meta's Ray-Bans and tailor it to what a BVI person might need – things like improved OCR and richly detailed scene and item descriptions. But after spending a few months with the EchoVision glasses, I found myself being more frustrated or disappointed instead of being impressed. Still, the impressive parts were impressive. Let's talk about it.

Getting Set Up

Setup had its moments. I used the QR code on the braille pamphlet to get to the user guide, though honestly a link to the EchoVision app itself would have been more helpful, since the guide lives in its own tab inside the app anyway. I also had trouble telling whether the device was even on. I was told I would hear a tone. Instead, a voice said "Glasses on," which was strange.

WiFi connected with no problem, and the scanning process was smooth. But after I powered the glasses off and picked them back up later, the app no longer recognized them. I had to forget the device and start over. And even then, I couldn't get VoiceOver audio through the glasses until I went into Bluetooth settings and set the glasses as headphones. After that, it worked fine — but nobody should have to discover that step on their own.

What I Liked

Reading Mode

The feature that really sets the EchoVision glasses apart from their competitors is the Reading Mode. The feature uses optical character recognition (OCR) through the glasses' camera. What sets it apart from telling your Metas to "Look and read," though, is the EchoVision's attention to the matter of page orientation. As a totally blind person, I have always had trouble figuring out if a piece of paper is entirely in frame. The EchoVision solve this brilliantly with verbal guidance, taking the picture only when the page is fully visible.

Another feature you won't find on Metas is the ability to move about your OCR'd documents using the EchoVision's touchpad. When reading official documents, this becomes useful for skipping past all the administrative information at the top and getting to the reason you're reading the thing. These are both really strong examples that people sat down and thought about. They found uses for the touchpad that are truly helpful for the BVI audience. But I do have my problems with the touchpad, but I'll get to them later.

Live AI

Just in the few months I've been testing them out, the Live AI feature on the EchoVisions has improved significantly, pushing it ahead of what Meta's Live AI is currently capable of doing--without third party intervention, anyway. The EchoVisions not only respond to your prompts in real time, but they automatically generate new descriptions based on changes to the live scene. These changes could be environmental, like a person moving or a door opening. Or they could be user-created, like when you move your head and the camera now has a different view of what's around you. It may not be perfect, but this constant stream of updates comes the closest to an AI sighted guide instead of the traditional point and shoot camera.

What I Didn't Like

The wake word: Where it at?

The first week I got the EchoVision glasses, I spent an embarrassing amount of time digging through the settings user guide, and assorted user groups. I scathed the internet high and low until my little cyber tootsies could handle no more. And so if I'm missing something, please feel free to correct me, but the EchoVision glasses do not support voice activation. If we look at the Metas, the only way to activate them is to say the wake word, "Hey Meta." With the EchoVision glasses, the only way to use the features is to use the different button combinations. Why is it one or the other? The perfect glasses would allow me to choose between using my voice or the buttons depending on my present situation. Imagine having your hands ful and needing a sudden scene description. Right now with the EchoVisions, you're basically out of luck unless you can pull off a demonstration in peak flexibility.

The buttons are confusing.

I'll be the one to say it: The buttons and touchpad on the EchoVisions is unnecessarily complicated (Insert Avril Lavigne music here). It's a level of cognitive load that more than a few folks won't be willing to carry. Just to illustrate my point, here is an exhaustive list of the controls.

  • The Camera Button (left side button)
    • Press it once – Take a picture
    • Press it twice – Start recording a video
    • Press it once while recording a video – Start recording video.
    • Press it three times – Announce battery and WiFi connectivity
    • Press and hold for 10 seconds – Power the glasses on and off
    • Press and hold for 30 seconds – Force a restart

But wait, there's also the right button.

  • AI Button – Right side button
    • Press it once – Scene description (EchoVision takes a picture and describes what's in front of you).
    • Press it twice – Live AI
    • Press it three times – Reading Mode.
    • Press five times – Send a system report with spoken feedback. This one is a genuine feature hidden behind so many presses!
    • Press and hold – Scene description with a custom prompt
    • Press six time – Bluetooth pairing mode
    • Press or hold while EchoVision is busy – Stop the current action
    • Press for thirty seconds – Factory reset

I had to take such a deep breath after all that, but that's still not including the touchpad.

  • Swipe forward (toward the lenses) along the line on the outside surface of the left arm to increase the volume.
  • Swipe backward (away from the lenses) along the line on the outside surface of the left arm to decrease the volume.
  • Single tap:
    • In Live AI mode: mute or unmute the microphone
    • In Q&A or Reading modes: pause or resume audio
    • During music playback: pause or resume
    • During phone call or Aira session: mute or unmute the microphone (Be My Eyes calls have no mute)
  • Double tap:
    • In Live AI: Refresh the current image and get a new description
    • In Reading mode: skip ahead to the next section
    • During phone call: answer or end the call
    • During Aira or Be My Eyes call: hang up
  • Triple tap:
    • In Q&A: replay the answer
    • In Reading mode: go back to the previous section
  • Long press:
    • If connected to a phone: trigger your phone's voice assistant (Siri or Google Assistant)
    • In Live AI, Reading, or Q&A: stop any AI interaction
    • During Aira call: turn the camera on or off

I've played modern video games with simpler controls than this thing. So again, I have to say it, why not include a voice assistant that would immediately take that cognitive load away from users?

You can't interrupt it

Here's another one. When Live AI is talking, you can't talk over it. If a description is running long and you want to jump in with a follow-up question, too bad — the glasses don't pick up your voice until the description finishes. For a feature that's supposed to feel like a sighted guide walking beside you, that's a problem, because real guides let you interrupt them.

There's a related quirk: when something small changes in the scene — an object comes into view, say — Live AI regenerates the whole scene description instead of just telling you the part that changed. A short "a door opened on your left" would do. Instead you get the full rundown again, and remember, you can't cut it off.

The hardware itself

For $600, I expected the glasses to feel like $600. They don't. The frames are lightweight plastic and lack the solid, premium feel of regular consumer eyewear — they feel more like a prototype than a finished product. The open-ear speakers are fine for short speech prompts, but they lack clarity and range. In a noisy outdoor environment, or if you wanted to listen to anything longer than a quick description, they fall short.

The Verdict

So where does that leave the EchoVision? The ideas at the center of it are genuinely good. Reading Mode's framing guidance solves a real, everyday problem for blind readers better than anything else I've tried, and Live AI's constant scene updates point at where this whole category should be heading.

But the value question is hard to get past. The EchoVision costs $600, with a subscription proposed on top of that. Meanwhile, Meta's Ray-Bans run about $299, and when you pair them with third-party accessibility apps like Scribe Me, you get comparable scene descriptions and environmental scanning for a lot less money. Against that, the EchoVision's advantages have to be enormous to justify paying twice — and right now, the complicated controls, the missing wake word, and the build quality eat away at the advantages it does have.

If Agiga adds voice activation, simplifies the controls down to something a person can actually memorize, and brings the hardware up to the level of the price tag, they'll have something special. The thinking behind these glasses is clearly done by people who understand blind users. I want the execution to catch up to the vision — pun fully intended.
